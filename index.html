The actual problem

Everyone in your niche is starting to look the same.

Same zooms. Same stock B-roll. Same three sound effects. Editors reach for volume because volume is easy to show and easy to charge for — and somewhere in all of it, the thing that made you worth watching gets buried.

Your content already has a feeling in it. The pause before the point. The line you got right on the third take. The place where you stopped performing and just said the thing. Most editing flattens that. It fills the gaps instead of using them.

I work the other way. I find what your content already is and I cut everything that stands in front of it. What people walk away with isn’t the edit — it’s how you made them feel. That’s the part that has to survive.

Rule no. 1If a cut doesn’t move the idea or the feeling forward, it doesn’t survive.

How it works

You’ll see the direction in writing before you commit to a month of it.

Six steps. Each one exists to remove a specific risk for you, not to look thorough.

A paid test edit

You send ten minutes of real footage. I cut it properly and you pay for the time. You see exactly how I think before either of us commits to anything longer.

The planner

A questionnaire about your audience, your references, what you want a viewer to feel, and what you can’t stand. It takes you twenty minutes and it prevents most of the revision rounds that would otherwise happen.

Research

I go through your back catalogue, your comments and the channels you admire. Not to copy them — to find where your work already differs and make that deliberate.

Style sheet and moodboard

A written document: pacing, typography, colour, sound, how cold opens start, what never gets used. Plus visual references. It’s yours to keep, and it’s what we both edit against.

We talk it through

One call on the style sheet before any retainer starts. You push back, I adjust, we agree the direction in writing. Then the agreement and scope get signed.

Retainer, feedback, refinement

You record, I cut, you leave timestamped notes. Every note goes back into the style sheet, so the same correction never happens twice. By month two you’re reviewing, not directing.

Before you ask

The questions people are too polite to ask first.

What if the first cut just isn’t what I pictured?
That’s the fear the whole process is built around. You see how I think on a paid test edit before you commit to anything bigger. Then we agree a written style sheet: pacing, tone, what never gets used. That way “what you pictured” is on paper before I cut a frame. If a cut misses something we agreed in writing, I fix it at no cost. That’s my mistake, not a revision.
Will my videos end up looking like everyone else’s?
No templates, no preset packs, no house style pasted over your channel. The style sheet is built from your content, your references and your audience, and it’s the only thing I edit against. If a zoom, a sound effect or a caption doesn’t serve the idea, it isn’t there.
Will I have to give the same notes every week?
No. Every note you give goes into the style sheet, so the same correction never has to happen twice. Most people start by directing and, within a month or two, are just reviewing.
How many revisions are included?
One full round of revisions is included on every edit. You leave timestamped comments on the cut and I work through all of them together. Rounds after that are billed by the time they take, and I tell you the estimate before I start, so nothing turns up on an invoice you didn’t agree to.
How do I know you won’t go quiet mid-project?
I work with a small number of clients at a time, deliberately. You get a delivery date when we start, and if anything is going to move you hear it from me before the deadline, not after. That’s the whole reason the paid test edit exists: you find out how I work on a small commitment, not a big one.
How does payment work?
Invoiced in USD, paid by PayPal or crypto. [ Deposit % ] to start and the balance on delivery, or monthly in advance on a retainer. You get an invoice every time.
Will you show my videos in your portfolio?
Yes, and I’m upfront about it because it’s a condition of working together: I show short excerpts of the work, and I say which parts of the edit were mine. I never show anything before your video is public. The video itself is yours, completely and permanently. You own it, you publish it, you do whatever you like with it. I only keep the right to show the work I did on it.
Have you edited for someone like me before?
Straight answer: most of my paid volume came from a different world. For years I was the edit partner to over a hundred American practice owners (dentists, through a marketing agency), publishing week after week with their own names on the work. It taught me deadlines, consistency, and how to cut for someone whose reputation is on the line rather than for a trend. The storytelling work you see here is where I’ve deliberately moved, and it’s the only work I take now. If you want to see the earlier catalogue, ask and I’ll send it.
Am I emailing a person or a company?
Just me. One editor, one point of contact, one person on the call. You can see my face and hear how I talk about your channel before you commit to anything.
