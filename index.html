The actual problem

Everyone in your niche is starting to look the same.

Same zooms. Same stock B-roll. Same three sound effects. Editors reach for volume because volume is easy to show and easy to charge for — and somewhere in all of it, the thing that made you worth watching gets buried.

Your content already has a feeling in it. The pause before the point. The line you got right on the third take. The place where you stopped performing and just said the thing. Most editing flattens that. It fills the gaps instead of using them.

I work the other way. I find what your content already is and I cut everything that stands in front of it. What people walk away with isn’t the edit — it’s how you made them feel. That’s the part that has to survive.

If a cut doesn’t move the idea or the feeling forward, it doesn’t survive.

How it works

You’ll see the direction in writing before you commit to a month of it.

Six steps. Each one exists to remove a specific risk for you, not to look thorough.

A paid test edit

You send ten minutes of real footage. I cut it properly and you pay for the time. You see exactly how I think before either of us commits to anything longer.

The planner

A questionnaire about your audience, your references, what you want a viewer to feel, and what you can’t stand. It takes you twenty minutes and it prevents most of the revision rounds that would otherwise happen.

Research

I go through your back catalogue, your comments and the channels you admire. Not to copy them — to find where your work already differs and make that deliberate.

Style sheet and moodboard

A written document: pacing, typography, colour, sound, how cold opens start, what never gets used. Plus visual references. It’s yours to keep, and it’s what we both edit against.

We talk it through

One call on the style sheet before any retainer starts. You push back, I adjust, we agree the direction in writing. Then the agreement and scope get signed.

Retainer, feedback, refinement

You record, I cut, you leave timestamped notes. Every note goes back into the style sheet, so the same correction never happens twice. By month two you’re reviewing, not directing.

Working together

How engagements are priced.

Per project

Priced per finished minute, scoped after I’ve seen your footage and understood what the video has to do. Effort doesn’t track runtime — a tight eight-minute cut can be harder than a loose forty — so I quote the project, not the clock.

Monthly capacity

Most people end up here. A fixed slot in my month covering long-form plus the shorts that come out of it, matched to how often you publish. I keep a small number of clients at a time, so capacity is genuinely limited.

Before you ask

The questions people are too polite to ask first.

My footage isn’t public yet. How do I know it’s safe?
Every project starts with an NDA if you want one — I’ll sign yours or send mine. Files move through access-controlled transfer, not open links in email. Only I touch them. Nothing appears here or anywhere else without your written sign-off. If you’re sitting on a launch or an embargoed interview, tell me the date and the whole workflow is built around it staying quiet until you say go.
What happens to my files if we stop working together?
They’re yours. Everything lives in a shared folder you have access to from day one — raw footage, project files, exports, the style sheet. If you move on, you take the folder and hand it to the next editor without losing a step. Nothing is held hostage, and I don’t keep your footage after you ask me to delete it.
Won’t the time difference slow everything down?
We won’t be on a live call every day, and we don’t need to be. You send footage and notes; I turn around a cut; you review on your own schedule with timestamped comments. There’s a set overlap window each week for anything that needs to be live, and anything urgent gets a reply the same working day. In practice you stop noticing it — you wake up to a finished cut instead of waiting on one.
How do I know you won’t go quiet mid-project?
I work with a small number of clients at a time, deliberately. You get a delivery date when we start, and if anything is going to move you hear it from me before the deadline, not after. That’s the whole reason the paid test edit exists — you find out how I work on a small commitment, not a big one.
